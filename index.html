§ 1 — The Legal Perimeter

Four doors, three of
them locked.
Know which is which.

Your working assumption — "I can't work on F-1, but OPT and STEM-OPT fix that" — is half right in a way that matters enormously. OPT fixes it. STEM OPT re-breaks it, and re-breaks it precisely for founders.

USCIS treats self-employment as employment. That cuts both ways: it means running your own company counts against the 90-day unemployment clock in your favour on OPT, and it means you cannot do it at all without authorization. On post-completion OPT, self-employment is permitted where three conditions hold together: you hold the proper business licenses and formation documents with an EIN, you are actively engaged in the business at 20+ hours per week, and your DSO agrees the business is related to your degree program. A biomedical-engineering PhD running an environmental-health sensing and data company clears that third test comfortably — better than almost any other business you could pick.

The 24-month STEM extension is a different regulation with a different logic. It exists to provide training, so it requires an employer that is enrolled in E-Verify, holds an EIN, and has the resources to deliver a structured training plan on Form I-983 — plus a bona fide employer-employee relationship. SEVP guidance names sole proprietorships, multiple-employer arrangements, and consulting-for-hire structures as arrangements that "may not be able to demonstrate" that relationship. ICE can conduct site visits, with 48 hours' notice, or with none at all if there is a complaint.

The cliff, stated plainly

Month 12 of OPT is not a renewal point. It is a structural discontinuity. Either your company can hire you through a genuine arm's-length employment relationship that survives ICE scrutiny — which effectively requires outside capital, an independent board, and other employees — or you must go work for someone else and reduce yourself to a passive owner of your own company for two years. Plan for the second and be delighted if you get the first.

Killed on sight: E-2

India has no E-2 treaty with the United States. The treaty-investor visa — the standard "buy yourself a job" route for foreign founders — is closed to you by citizenship, not by residency or by money. The commonly-marketed workaround is acquiring a second citizenship in a treaty country such as Grenada or Türkiye. That is a six-figure exercise and out of scope at your capital level. Stop reading E-2 content.

Killed on sight: SBIR/STTR

The obvious "get funded by the EPA/NIH for exactly the thing you already do" move fails an ownership test, not a merit test. SBA rules require more than 50% direct ownership by US citizens or permanent residents, measured fully diluted. As sole foreign-national owner you are ineligible. You can participate as a minority owner, as an STTR research partner, or as a subcontractor — but you cannot be the majority-owner applicant. This kills a lot of otherwise-attractive plans.

Deferred, not dead: IER

The International Entrepreneur Rule grants parole to founders who raise at least $311,071 from qualified US investors, or $124,429 in qualified government grants, for filings on or after 1 Oct 2024. Re-parole demands $622,142 raised, or $622,142 annual revenue with 20%+ growth. At sub-$5K starting capital this is not a Year-1 instrument. It is a Year-3 instrument if the venture path works — and worth designing toward.

The single highest-leverage immigration fact in this document is not about your company at all. It is that university H-1B petitions are cap-exempt — no lottery, filed any month of the year — and UT Dallas is exactly such an employer.Implication — a postdoc or research-scientist appointment at UTD after your defense converts the 12-month OPT sprint into a multi-year runway, during which you may own the company passively while a co-founder or first employee operates it, and during which you accumulate the publications, press and independent-judging record that an O-1A actually requires.

§ 2 — Asset Audit

You are not an
ML person who
touched hardware.
You are a metrologist.

This distinction is the entire commercial argument of this brief, so it is worth being precise about it.

There are perhaps a hundred thousand people in the United States who can train a model on a time series. There are a very large number who can wire a sensor to a microcontroller. There is a much smaller population who has designed the enclosure in CAD, machined it, specified the PCB, integrated the sensors over I²C and UART, powered it off-grid, backhauled it over LoRaWAN, ingested it into InfluxDB, rendered it in Grafana, and — the rare part — calibrated the whole assembly against EPA reference-grade instrumentation and published the result in a peer-reviewed journal.

That last clause is the moat. Low-cost air sensors are a commodity; the industry's open secret is that monitors from $169 to $6,000 use the same particulate modules. What separates a $169 device from a $6,000 device is not the sensing element. It is traceability — the defensible claim that the number on the screen means something. Traceability is produced by collocation studies, calibration models, drift correction and documented method. That is what you have spent three years doing, and it is the part almost nobody can buy off a shelf.

Layer on top of that the regulatory coursework nobody expects a sensor engineer to have. GMP with an A. Introduction to Medical Device Product Development with an A. Experimental Methods and Statistical Analysis with an A. A graduate certificate in Development and Regulation of Medical Devices and Drugs. Six Sigma Yellow Belt and AI in Biomedical Engineering in progress. Physiology and Immunology for Engineers. You are, unusually, a person who can build the instrument, defend its numbers statistically, and write the quality documentation for it.

§ 3c — The Markets This Brief Originally Missed

Air quality was
too small a frame.Here is the rest.

The first draft of this research assumed your market was air quality because that is what your dissertation is about. That was a mistake of imagination. What you actually own is traceable measurement in environments where being wrong is expensive — and there are at least six such environments within driving distance of Richardson, most of them richer than air quality.

Federal procurement data makes the point bluntly. Across 53 air-quality and environmental-sensing awards since January 2024, agencies obligated $5.69M — an average of $107K per award, with a maximum of $1.30M. That is a real market and a servable one for a single operator. But three findings inside that data matter more than the headline.

First, the money is in open competition, not set-asides. Forty-seven of the 53 awards, carrying $4.70M of the $5.69M, were coded "No Set-Aside Used." Only four awards totalling $158K went through Total Small Business. This is the single most important commercial fact in this revision: SBIR is closed to you on ownership grounds, and that felt like it closed the government channel. It does not. The overwhelming majority of these dollars flow through ordinary competitive procurement, where the ownership test that kills SBIR does not apply the same way. Verify the foreign-ownership and FOCI position with counsel before bidding — but do not write the channel off, as the first draft implicitly did.

Second, the winning NAICS codes are services, not hardware. Testing Laboratories (541380) took $1.75M and Environmental Consulting (541620) took $1.63M — together 59% of the pool — against $1.02M for measuring-device manufacturing. The government is buying judgement and documentation, not boxes. PurpleAir did win an EPA Region 10 purchase order, but for $23,328; Eastern Research Group, a services firm, took $2.50M across three awards.

Third, Texas is nearly absent from the public-sector air market. Of 200 state and local air-monitoring opportunities in the database, the leaders are Massachusetts (26), New York (19), Pennsylvania (17), West Virginia (15), Colorado and DC (12 each). Texas does not appear in the top ten. Your local public sector is not a customer. Your local private sector, however, is extraordinary.

§ 5 — What Nobody Tells You Until It Costs You

The nine things that
actually decide this.

Everything above is the map. This is the terrain. Each item below is either a hidden cost, a regulatory edge case, or a second-order effect that surfaces only after you have committed.

01 — Your university may own your idea

UT Dallas operates under UT System Regents' Rule 90101. A student owns IP created in a course. But the Board may assert ownership over intellectual property created by persons employed by UTD, by persons — including students — using UTD facilities under the supervision of its employees, and specifically by candidates for master's and doctoral degrees. You are all three of those things simultaneously.

The practical consequence: a calibration methodology developed on MINTS Lab hardware, under Dr. Lary's supervision, on a project funded by lab grants, is plausibly university IP. A calibration methodology you develop on your own equipment, on your own time, in a domain distinct from your dissertation, is plausibly yours. The line is real and it is not drawn by your intentions — it is drawn by facilities, supervision and funding source.

Do this first, before anything else

Book a meeting with the UTD Office of Research and Innovation / Technology Commercialization before you form an entity. Ask for a written determination on the boundary between your dissertation work and a proposed commercial activity, and ask whether a conflict-of-interest management plan is required. Doing this early costs a week. Doing it late can cost you the company — and, worse, your relationship with your advisor and your defense timeline.

02 — The DSO is a discretionary gate, not a form

"Related to your degree program" is not defined by statute in a way that produces certainty. Your DSO decides. A biomedical-engineering PhD proposing an environmental-exposure and health-data company has an easy case; the same student proposing a general software agency does not. This asymmetry should shape which business you pick as much as the market does.

03 — The 90-day clock is unforgiving and self-inflicted

Ninety cumulative days of unemployment — or ninety days without reporting employment — and SEVP terminates the SEVIS record within 15 days. Reporting must happen in the SEVP Portal within 10 days of any change. Self-employment counts as employment, but only if you report it, and only at 20+ hours per week. The most common founder failure here is not lack of revenue; it is a founder who was working 60 hours a week on their company and never filed the portal entry. Report on day one of OPT with the business already formed, licensed and EIN'd — not on the day you land your first customer.

04 — Texas taxes your software but not your advice

Texas treats SaaS as a taxable data-processing service under Comptroller Rule 3.330, applying the 80/20 rule: roughly 80% of the charge is taxable, 20% exempt as an information service. Effective all-in rate lands near 6.6%. Professional consulting services are generally not taxed the same way. The structural implication is direct: a $30K consulting engagement carries no sales-tax exposure; the identical value delivered as a $30K software subscription does. Split your invoices deliberately from day one — retroactive reallocation during an audit is the expensive version of this lesson.

Franchise tax is a non-issue at your scale: the 2026 no-tax-due threshold is $2.65M annualized revenue. You still must file a Public Information Report or Ownership Information Report every year. Missing that filing is a $50 penalty and a forfeited charter — a trivial cost that has killed real companies through administrative dissolution at the worst moment.

05 — The calibration-transfer gap is a real research problem, which is why it is a real business

EPA's own guidance recommends collocating at least three identical sensors with FRM/FEM monitors for a minimum of 30 days at two sites in different climatic regions. That is expensive, slow, and geographically impossible for most deployments. The open problem — actively worked in the 2025–2026 literature on calibration transfer, distance-based uncertainty quantification and unsupervised correction — is how to apply a calibration to a sensor deployed far from any reference station.

Nobody has commercialised the answer well. Clarity bundles collocation and custom calibration free into a hardware subscription, which means it is a cost centre subsidising hardware margin, not a product. That is your wedge: sell calibration transfer as a service to everyone else's hardware, including the open-source fleet. It is defensible, it is genuinely hard, it is exactly your dissertation-adjacent expertise, and it is software.

06 — Compliance is the only durable wedge in IAQ

Voluntary monitoring gets cancelled in the first budget cycle. Compliance monitoring does not. RESET Air requires continuous monitoring, an accredited monitor, annual calibration, submission through a RESET Accredited Data Provider to the RESET Assessment Cloud, and daily averages held within limits for three consecutive months. WELL v2 recognises RESET-certified monitors. ASHRAE 241 is voluntary until a jurisdiction adopts it or a contract requires it — and contracts increasingly require it.

Read that list again as a business model. "Annual calibration" is a recurring revenue line written into a standard. "Accredited data provider" is a licensing moat with a finite number of holders. The certification bodies have designed your subscription for you.

07 — The AI-services trap is real and quantified

Roughly 90% of AI-wrapper startups are projected to fail by end of 2026, with 60–70% generating zero revenue, and wrappers run 25–35% gross margins against 70–85% for genuine SaaS. The survivors are vertical, encode domain knowledge that took years to acquire, and charge early. The $20K-MRR-solo-founder stories that circulate are real but heavily survivorship-biased — treat them as existence proofs, not base rates.

The correct use of AI agents in your case is not as the product. It is as the delivery mechanism: agents that draft calibration reports, generate V&V protocol boilerplate, monitor sensor drift and raise exceptions, and handle first-line customer communication — so that one person services fifteen accounts. Sell metrology and regulatory judgement; let agents absorb the document production.

08 — Insurance, contracts and the boring things that end consultancies

Errors-and-omissions cover is not optional the moment you sign anything that says the word "calibration" next to the word "compliance." A client who fails a certification and blames your calibration model is a foreseeable event. Budget $800–2,000/yr for E&O plus general liability at this scale. Every contract needs a limitation-of-liability clause capped at fees paid, an explicit disclaimer that you are not a licensed professional engineer, and a clause disclaiming responsibility for regulatory outcomes. If you ever drift toward mold or asbestos, Texas requires TDLR licensure and $1M per-occurrence general liability — a different business with a different balance sheet.

09 — Banking and formation as a non-resident is solved, cheaply

This is the part people expect to be hard and isn't. Form the entity, obtain an EIN via Form SS-4 without an SSN — 4–8 business days through a formation service, up to several months if you file cold — then open Mercury or similar with your Certificate of Formation, EIN confirmation letter (CP575 or 147C), signed operating agreement and passport. An ITIN follows for tax filing. Total cost is in the hundreds, not thousands.

The real decision is LLC versus Delaware C-corp, and it is a fork with immigration consequences. An LLC is simpler and cheaper and is the right default for a services business. A Delaware C-corp is required if you ever want institutional investment — which is the precondition for the IER path, for a credible O-1A petition from your own company, and for an H-1B structure with an independent board. If the "scalable startup" objective is genuine, the C-corp is the correct answer despite the extra paperwork.

The absence test: across every source consulted for this brief, there is almost no published account of an F-1 founder who successfully navigated the STEM OPT cliff while retaining operational control.Interpretation — the silence is not evidence that it cannot be done. It is evidence that it is done through structures that lawyers do not advertise and founders do not blog about, or that it is simply not done and those founders quietly took jobs. Either way, plan on the assumption that month 13 requires a different structure than month 12, and treat any advisor who tells you otherwise as a source of risk.

§ 7 — Red Team

The strongest case
against everything
above.

A brief that only argues one side is marketing. Here is the argument a hostile, well-informed reader would make.

The structural argument

The OPT founder path is a 12-month window with a hard stop, a 90-day clock, and a discretionary gatekeeper. Even if everything goes right, at month 13 you must either raise institutional capital, find an E-Verify employer willing to hire you while you own a competing entity, or become a passive owner of a business that needs an operator you cannot afford. The base rate for "solo technical founder builds a services business to institutional-investment scale in twelve months while finishing a PhD" is very low. The honest expected outcome is that you build a profitable side consultancy, take a job at month 13, and let it idle. That is not failure — but it is a materially different plan than "start a company," and you should price it that way.

The market argument

Air-quality monitoring is a crowded, commoditising market where an open-source competitor publishes its full BOM and undercuts on price by 3–5×. The market-size figures in § 3 come from commercial research vendors whose estimates for the same category vary by a factor of 2.5, which means they are marketing artifacts as much as measurements. Indoor air quality had a demand spike in 2020–2022 that has since substantially normalised; ASHRAE 241 remains voluntary in most jurisdictions. You may be entering a category on the back side of its hype cycle.

The credentials argument

On O-1A: three journal articles published in the same year in MDPI titles, one conference presentation, no major awards, no documented peer-review or judging service, no press coverage, no patents. That is a strong PhD student. It is not, today, a comfortable O-1A file. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling petition services. The gap is closable in 18–24 months with deliberate effort — journal reviewing, conference program committees, press, patents — but it is a gap, and pretending otherwise leads to a denied petition and a status problem.

What would have to be true for the opposite conclusion?

  • That a services business can reach ~$150–250K of annualised revenue inside 12 months on relationship sales alone. Plausible at $150–300/hr consulting rates with 3–5 retained clients. This is the load-bearing assumption of the whole plan.
  • That UTD grants a cap-exempt research appointment post-defense. Largely within your control — it depends on your advisor and funding, both of which you can influence starting now.
  • That the calibration-transfer wedge is genuinely under-served rather than merely under-marketed. Uncertain. Test it with five customer conversations before writing code.
  • That compliance-driven IAQ demand keeps growing independent of pandemic salience. Supported by RESET/WELL/LEED structural requirements and by the QMSR transition, which are contractual rather than sentimental.

Biases I am carrying, declared

Survivorship. Founder-success sources skew hard toward winners; there is no registry of the F-1 students who quietly folded. Source. Much of the immigration guidance available online comes from law firms and visa-services companies with an incentive to describe difficult paths as achievable. Where possible I have weighted toward USCIS, ICE/SEVP, NAFSA and university international-office material. Recency. The 2025 H-1B modernization rule and the Feb 2026 QMSR transition are recent enough that practitioner experience with both is thin — I may be overweighting them. Vendor. Every market-size number here originates with a firm that sells market reports.

Where the conclusion survives

The red team damages the venture framing badly and the services framing barely at all. A one-person, zero-inventory, high-rate technical consultancy in calibration and regulatory documentation is robust to almost every objection above: it needs no capital, it reaches revenue in weeks, it satisfies the DSO relatedness test, it satisfies the 90-day clock, it generates the case-study and press evidence an O-1A needs, and it costs nothing to mothball at month 13 if you take a job. Start there. Everything else is an option you buy with its cash flow.

§ 8 — Decision Points

Four forks. Each
with its real price.

Not recommendations — cleanly framed choices with consequences attached, so you can make them yourself.

Fork 1 — Entity form

LLC → cheapest, simplest, pass-through, right for services. Sacrifices institutional investability, which forecloses IER and weakens an own-company O-1A or H-1B petition.

Delaware C-corp → costs a few hundred more per year and real paperwork. Buys optionality on every venture and self-petition path.

If "scalable startup" is genuinely ranked first, this is a C-corp. If it is aspirational and services are the real plan, LLC and convert later.

Fork 2 — Timing of formation

Form now, operate later → entity, EIN, licences and banking all in place so you can report employment on day 1 of OPT. Requires absolute discipline about doing zero work for it in the interim, including no customer conversations.

Form at OPT approval → zero ambiguity, but burns 3–6 weeks of your 12-month window on administration and starts the 90-day clock while you wait for an EIN.

Form now. The discipline is the cheaper cost.

Fork 3 — Month 13

Cap-exempt UTD appointment → highest probability, preserves everything, slowest wealth creation. You own the company; someone else operates it.

E-Verify employer + passive ownership → conventional, safe, and you lose operational control for 24 months.

Own-company O-1A or H-1B with independent board → highest ceiling, requires outside capital and real governance, and is the path most likely to fail on evidence.

Pursue the first, prepare the second, engineer toward the third.

Fork 4 — What you sell first

Pharma validation & data integrity → highest rate, largest market, live catalyst, uses your whole transcript. But you are credentialed by coursework, not scars.

DFW critical-facility monitoring → local, tangible, your published expertise, buyers with real budgets. But you enter as a subcontractor in a relationship trade.

Calibration services → your deepest technical edge and smallest buyer pool.

Run the first two in parallel for 90 days. Whichever signs first is the company; the others become service lines.

§ 11 — Sources & Confidence

What I trust,
and how much.

Overall confidence: HIGH on the legal architecture, MEDIUM on market sizing, MEDIUM-LOW on revenue modelling.

High confidence. The OPT/STEM OPT self-employment distinction, the 90-day and 150-day unemployment limits, E-2 ineligibility for Indian nationals, SBIR majority-ownership requirements, IER dollar thresholds, the January 2025 H-1B modernization rule permitting >50% beneficiary-owners to petition with 18-month initial validity, Texas franchise-tax thresholds and the SaaS 80/20 rule, and RESET Air's structural requirements. These come from USCIS, ICE/SEVP, SBA, the Texas Comptroller, NAFSA, university international offices, and the standards bodies themselves, and multiple independent sources agree.

Medium confidence. Market sizing. Commercial research vendors produce a 2.5× spread on "indoor air quality monitoring" depending on category definition. The direction — growth in the 7–12% CAGR band — is consistent across sources; the absolute numbers are not. The regulatory-affairs outsourcing figures and the FDA authorization counts are firmer.

Medium-low confidence. The revenue scenarios in Fig. 10. Rate benchmarks are well sourced; the assumption that you specifically can close 3–5 retained clients in twelve months with no prior commercial sales experience is an estimate, and the largest single uncertainty in this document.

Thin or contradictory. Practitioner accounts of F-1 founders past the STEM OPT cliff; whether the calibration-transfer market is under-served or merely under-marketed; DFW-specific building and district counts, which I could not source reliably at the metro level and have therefore not quantified.
